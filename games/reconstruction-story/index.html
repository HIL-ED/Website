A New South?
A Reconstruction Story

You will not just read about it. You will live one version of it, choice by choice, and see where it leads.

In April 1865, the Civil War ends. Four million enslaved Americans are free. What that freedom will actually mean — who can vote, own land, hold office, testify in court, or simply walk down a road unafraid — is not settled by that single fact. Over the next twelve years, formerly enslaved people, white Southerners, federal officials, and the U.S. Congress argue, vote, build, and sometimes attack one another over that exact question.

This is a story about that fight, told from the ground up. You will choose who you are: a federal Freedmen's Bureau agent sent to help enforce freedom's promises with almost no soldiers to back you up, a white Southern politician trying to build an interracial democracy in a region that mostly resents you for it, or a formerly enslaved person building a life — a family, a farm, a vote, maybe even a seat in office — that the law never let you have before. You will make real decisions at real turning points, examine primary sources from the archive along the way, and pause periodically to reflect on what you're seeing.

One thing this story will not do: ask you to act out violence, or make you the target of a graphic scene. Moments of danger and intimidation are described as history — things that happened, reported plainly — not things you perform. Your choices are always about how to respond. And there is no score to win: the nation's larger story ends the way it actually did. How your own character met that ending is still yours to decide.

For Teachers: Standards, Timing & Content Approach ▾

Standards

Built around the C3 Framework's historical and civic dimensions (D2.His.1–3: change, continuity, and causation; D2.Civ.1, 10, 12: civic participation, deliberation, and the reach of constitutional rights) and commonly aligns with 7th/8th grade U.S. History units covering Reconstruction (1865–1877), the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments, federalism, and the roots of Jim Crow.

Timing

One full role takes about 25–35 minutes, including the primary-source archive and reflection prompts. Assign one role per student or table group, then compare epilogues as a whole-class discussion — all three roles are built to reach the same historical turning points from different vantage points, which makes for strong compare-and-contrast debriefs.

How this game handles difficult history

Reconstruction can't be taught honestly without the Black Codes, the Ku Klux Klan, and the violent campaigns that ended it, and this game includes all of that — but students are never asked to act out, narrate, or personally experience violence. Danger and intimidation are described in historical summary, in primary-source documents, or as the reported consequence of a choice; every decision a student makes is about how their character responds, never about carrying out harm. The national outcome (the Compromise of 1877 and the rise of Jim Crow) is historically fixed no matter what choices are made — only the character's personal story varies — so the point is never to "beat" the actual history.
